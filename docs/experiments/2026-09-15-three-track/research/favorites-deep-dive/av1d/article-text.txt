i’ve always struggled with image prompting. the prompts people share often feel made up: a pile of impressive words, followed by a beautiful result, with no explanation of which decisions mattered.

when my result looks wrong, that gives me very little to work with.Thank you.

so we built a workflow around making the visual decisions visible. decide what belongs in the image, describe how the parts relate, generate it, then check what survived

## start by deciding what the image must do

“make it premium” expresses a preference. you still have to decide what someone will see.

for a fashion app, that might mean generous spacing, consistent product photography, quiet typography, and a bag that looks identical across screens. 

for a perfume campaign, it might mean garnet glass, a walnut cap, believable reflections, and enough contrast to read the label.

write the brief in this order:

1. purpose: what should someone understand, feel, or do?

2. canvas: where will it appear, and what shape must it have?

3. contents: which subjects, objects, and exact words belong in it?

4. relationships: where are they, what touches what, and what comes first?

5. appearance: what should the materials, light, color, and typography look like?

6. checks: what would make the result unusable, even if it looks beautiful?

every sentence should make a visible decision or protect one.

> a useful prompt names objects, gives the right attributes to each object, and explains their relationships. 

“an oxblood bag beside a cream cardholder” binds color to a particular object. “the bag sits above its name and price” adds a relationship you can inspect. 

compositional benchmarks treat attribute binding and object relationships as distinct challenges.

## create a new image by building its hierarchy


review what the design means, as well as how it looks.

for your own app, write the screen roles first. for a poster, write the reading order. for a product image, decide the product’s size, placement, and relationship to the light.

the accompanying examples let you practice that translation across formats:

- book covers and posters: specify the title, visual metaphor, reading order, and clear margins;

- flyers and advertisements: separate the message, product, supporting information, and action;

- icons and logos: define silhouette, stroke consistency, small-size readability, and background requirements;

- headshots: preserve identity while changing wardrobe, framing, or light;

- interiors and cross-sections: distinguish visible design from hidden construction you are asking the model to invent.

our SABLE app started with three screen roles: discovery, collection, and product detail.

then we specified the product inventory, prices, selected controls, materials, and recurring bag design. those decisions give the model an interface to compose.

## reconstruct a reference with a visual map

when you already have an image, start by inventorying it.

we mapped the VELLUM photograph into separately named controls. this lets you identify the walnut cap, paper label, glass highlights, and reflection without describing the entire scene again.

turn your reference into a set of choices.

- name the parts. canvas → groups → objects → editable parts → properties. group tiny details until you need individual control.

- start big. shapes and empty spaces first. relationships next. materials and exact text last.

- flag uncertainty. separate visible facts, inferred details, and unknowns. bounds are approximate; crops may include neighboring pixels.

- choose the changes. keep, restyle, replace, remove, or move. tell the agent what must stay unchanged.

- hand off the plan. export a prompt and reconstruction JSON. the source image stays untouched.

name the parts. make the decisions reusable.


keep the original image as your source of truth.

our map adds bounding boxes and crop previews without changing it. asking AI to redraw the image with numbered labels can change the subject and make the map misleading.

- prepare: have your agent inspect the image and create reconstruction JSON.

- import: load the JSON and attach the matching clean image.

- verify: check every name and box before exporting.

the page doesn’t automatically detect or segment objects. section 8 explains when to use comments, maps, or sketches.

## save the visual decisions as reconstruction JSON

> the JSON separates what is visible, what someone wants to change, and how the result will be checked.

the full document includes the other elements, relationships, source file, unknowns, and checks. it does not recover the original prompt, source layers, exact font, or hidden geometry.

for a close recreation, prioritize silhouette, proportions, spacing, and relationships. then refine surface detail. for a new composition using selected ingredients, make that goal explicit before compiling.

keep observations separate from requests. the original cap remains walnut in the source inventory even if someone later asks for black metal. that request belongs in a selection record, with its own preservation rules and updated checks

think of three connected records:

keep the full record. send only what the edit needs.

- resolve conflicts. preserve source observations. when requesting a black cap, remove the old brown-cap instruction.

- review the compiler’s flags. it creates instructions, not masks or recovered layers. coordinates remain approximate.

- attach the image. a file path alone supplies no pixels.

- keep edits focused. save the inventory for reconstruction and handoff. compile a shorter request for local changes.

our 82,283-character JSON exceeded a 32,000-character limit before generation. that was an input-size failure, not an image-quality result.

## give every reference a specific job

references can define a person, a product, a layout, a material, or a palette. name those roles so the model has less to guess.

our OFFDAY packaging supplied the brand identity for a related advertisement and merchandise. the new output changes; the recognizable wordmark and emblem should carry through.

give every reference a job.

- assign roles. list each image in a table: identity, layout, or finish. specify what to borrow and ignore.

- resolve conflicts. choose the crop and light direction. say whether a texture reference can contribute objects or lettering.

- supply evidence. a palette cannot establish someone’s identity. a front view cannot reveal the back of a package.

- handle missing details. request another view when hidden information matters. otherwise, label new details as design choices.

## choose comments, the visual map, or a sketch

use the interface that communicates the difficult decision most directly.

point at the change. sketch the arrangement.

- use a comment when the target is obvious. select the cap and describe the edit. this is a reusable example, not another generation we ran:

> change this cap to charcoal black. preserve its wood grain, silhouette, height, position, and connection to the collar. keep the bottle, label, framing, and lighting direction unchanged. allow the cap’s highlights and reflection to adjust to the darker color.

the selection identifies the target. your words define change → preserve → allow.

- use the map when choices need to carry forward. record that comment against A-05 so another agent knows which part you meant. the map keeps multiple choices and preservation rules together.

inspect the result after either approach. unselected areas can still change.

- use a sketch when placement is hard to explain. draw rectangles, silhouettes, a horizon, and arrows. show where things belong, their relative sizes, what overlaps, and the viewpoint.

the drawing only needs to make the arrangement clear.

for our Coast House example, we attached the interior from section 2 and asked for the same room as an architectural sketch. the reference supplied the room design; the prompt changed how it was represented.

draw the placement. describe the finish.

our Coast House result kept the viewpoint, joinery, fireplace, artwork, sofa, chair, table, window seat, and coast. the shading was denser than the selective wash we requested.

this tested photograph → sketch. it doesn’t show that reversing the process would reproduce the photograph.

when starting with your own sketch:

- set the frame. match its shape to the final image. block in the largest silhouettes and useful empty spaces.

- show the structure. mark the viewpoint, horizon or floor, and overlaps. distinguish guide lines from object outlines. for actions, show torso direction, the planted foot, gaze, and where the hand touches an object.

- label what matters. add a short legend identifying the objects and arrows. specify which construction lines, labels, arrows, and guide colors must disappear.

- give the sketch the layout role. describe materials, lighting, texture, and finish separately. say whether the model may add objects.

- check placement before realism. if the window lands on the wrong wall, fix the drawing or spatial instruction first.

OpenAI’s @Sketch workflow combines drawing with a written request. use it where available, or upload a drawing or photograph of paper. our drawing-input experiments used uploads; we didn’t separately test the native drawing toolbar or authenticated image comments. 

keep the sketch simple enough that the intended structure stays obvious.

- isolate to diagnose. change one variable to understand its effect.

- combine compatible edits you can check together. sequence changes that depend on earlier results.

- write change → preserve → allow. protect unrelated content; allow necessary shadows and reflections.

- return to the best accepted source. don’t carry accidental damage forward.

## use agents to explore controlled alternatives

sub-agents can prepare reference combinations, write candidate briefs, or review results independently.

give each branch the same intended output and checklist. 

vary one meaningful input, such as the reference set, composition, or amount of lighting detail. fix the budget before generation. 

a larger search budget can improve the chance of finding an attractive candidate without proving the underlying prompt is better.

- coordinator: lock the source, label text, output shape, and checks.

- generators: record exact prompts, ordered attachments, settings, outputs, and changed visual evidence. generate sequentially if needed.

- reviewer: inspect actual images under neutral filenames. keep failures and disagreements.

- budget: cap candidates. separate initial results from repair costs.

- acceptance: check improvements and new damage against the prior best.
